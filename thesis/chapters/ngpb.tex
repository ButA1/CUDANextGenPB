\chapter{NextGenPB}
\label{sec:nextgenpb}

At the time of writing, Next Generation Poisson-Boltzmann (NextGenPB or NGPB for short) \cite{diflorio2025nextgenpb} is the most recent innovation in the field of LPBE solvers, aiming to improve accuracy while maximizing computational efficiency. 
It is purposefully modular and built upon free and open libraries, making improvements to individual steps easier, though, as I will explain later, this has to be taken with a grain of salt.
It is a family member of the grid- or FEM-based solvers, featuring local (de)refinement using the p4est library.
It uses MPI and MPI-aware mathematical libraries like BIMPP and LIS to scale across parallel architectures, lending itself well to high-performance computing.
Its main attractions are the use of the NanoShaper library for surface reconstruction and an enhanced analytical approach, allowing for much more accurate energy calculation, beating out many older LPBE solvers in both accuracy and speed.
This is an impressive feat that has gained the attention of biomolecular chemists at our technical university, who seek to replace their existing PBE solver APBS \cite{jurrus2018apbs} with NGPB.
% yes I actually put that em dash there

To avoid the self-energy singularity, NGPB computes the electrostatic energies individually, where each of them is never evaluated at the atom's center.
This is an entirely new approach, which avoids reformulating, doing additional differencing or using a different model other than point charges.
Thus the total electrostatic energy NGPB computes is a sum of the Coulomb, polarization and ionic energy.

\section{Libraries}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries}

\subsection{NanoShaper}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-nanoshaper}

NanoShaper \cite{abate2025nanoshaperweb} is a surface reconstruction and pocket detection tool aimed at biomolecular systems.
It is compatible with patch-based, implicit or triangulated surfaces and works by casting rays in a grid pattern, computing both surface intersections and normals. 
The authors of NGPB leverage this by aligning the grid of their problem space with that of NanoShaper, giving them the necessary surface information essentially for free.

\subsection{P4est}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-p4est}

P4est \cite{carsten_burstedde_2024_10839051} is a library for dynamic management of adaptive quadtrees, while octrees are taken care of by its submodule p8est.
It is designed for parallel work and scales well on many-core systems, though unfortunately, it has not been officially adapted to work on GPUs.
NGPB uses it for creating and adapting octrees by refining, coarsening, and enforcing 2:1 conditions between neighbors. It also uses it to partition the octree between MPI processes and handle the communication between them.

\subsection{BIMPP}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-bimpp}

BIMPP \cite{defalco_bimpp} is an undocumented library written by one of the co-authors of NGPB. 
NGPB does not use the standard version of it, but rather a special ``NextGenPB-v0.0.01'' release, which contains additional operators meant specifically for NGPB.
Due to the lack of documentation, it is difficult to get an overview of all its features.
I would describe it as a library that enables distributed vector- and sparse-matrix operations on quad- and octrees.
It itself is based on p4est, and it too handles the communication of non-local elements between the MPI processes.

In order to perform parallel computations on a sparse matrix, they are broken up into segments, where each node (or process) handles one of them locally.
However, this creates an issue where some operations would affect non-local entries of segments that belong to another node.
Those non-local changes need to be communicated after each operation.
The current implementation is known to be memory inefficient. 
The main culprit is the way BIMPP stores its distributed sparse matrix. 
For every rank, BIMPP holds both local and non-local values of a distributed sparse matrix in a \snakecase{std::vector} of \snakecase{std::map}, where the \snakecase{std::vector} is sized to the global amount of rows.
\snakecase{std::vector} initializes an empty map for every entry.
This means that matrix rows that don't even need to track any non-locals still initialize an empty \snakecase{std::map}.
This is particularly painful when the global amount of rows is very large while also launching a lot of MPI ranks, as the total memory usage increases with each additional rank.

\subsubsection{bim3a\_laplacian\_frac}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-bimpp-bim3a-laplacian-frac}

This is one of the proprietary functions only found in the NGPB-specific release I mentioned, and a critical step in the numeric solution.
Its purpose is to build the laplacian matrix operator by mapping a function to compute the intersection point $\alpha$, to then evaluate the effective electric permittivity $\varepsilon^{eff}$ using the weighted harmonic average (WHA) of the permittivity of the solvent and the molecule $\varepsilon_{1}$ and $\varepsilon_{2}$.

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon^{eff} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{\alpha}{\varepsilon_1} + \dfrac{(1-\alpha)}{\varepsilon_2}}
    \label{eq:effective-permittivity}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{bim3a\_solution\_with\_ghosts}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-bimpp-bim3a-solution-with-ghosts}

This is the function that handles the communication of non-locals of distributed vectors computed from the octree mesh, between the ranks and is used many times throughout NGPB, like for example \snakecase{create_markers} and \snakecase{create_density_map}.

During my work on the thesis, it became increasingly obvious that this function took way longer than it should.
This function rediscovers non-local and ghost quadrants of the octree that each rank can see, running costly updates on quadrants multiple times.

It then finally calls \snakecase{remap()}, which collects all the non-local values from the other ranks, and \snakecase{assemble()}, which distributes the updated values.

\subsection{LIS}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-lis}

The library of Iterative Solvers for linear systems \cite{lis_library} is an MPI- and OpenMP-aware (and therefore parallel) software library for solving linear equations and eigenvalue problems using iterative methods.
It supports both dense matrices as well as CSR matrices.
As is typical for a linear software library, it offers many different solver methods and preconditioners.

\subsection{MUMPS}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-mump}

The multifrontal massively parallel sparse direct solver \cite{mumps_solver} is an alternative solver available in NGPB.
Unlike LIS this is a direct solver.
It not only accepts dense and CSR matrices, but it can also accept dictionary formats.
It uses LU factorization to simplify the matrix.

\section{Submodules}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules}

In this section I want to showcase the structure of flow of NGPB.
It can be roughly summarized into the following stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reading inputs
    \item Grid \& surface construction
    \item Matrix assembly
    \item Solving the linear equation
    \item Energy calculation
\end{enumerate}
Their time complexity can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:original-profiling}.
The two most time consuming parts for large numbers of atoms are clearly the linear equation solve, and the energy calculation.
The energy calculation also exhibits a non-linear growth.

\subsection{Reading Inputs}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-input}

NGPB reads in settings and parameters via PRM files, which are given as an argument to the \snakecase{ngpb} command.
These settings include the path to the molecule, mesh settings, simulation parameters and algorithmic settings.

\subsubsection{Mesh Settings}

NGPB offers four different mesh types, on which the remaining mesh parameters depend \cite{nextgenpb_docs}. 
The first and default option is \snakecase{mesh_shape = 0}, which is a de-refined mesh with a fine and coarse region.
The parameters \snakecase{perfil1} and \snakecase{perfil2} (short for ``percentage fill'') set the fraction of the fine and coarse domain size occupied by the molecule, so the higher the value, the tighter the corresponding region wraps around it. 
Their default values are 0.8 and 0.2 respectively.

Logically, one would want the molecule to be fully inside the fine grid domain but also leave enough room for the Stern layer. This is a thin layer only a few Ångströms thick that exists on the surface of solids submerged in a solution, in which charged particles behave differently.
The inclusion of the Stern layer is optional, and controlled through the \snakecase{stern_layer_surf} parameter and its thickness with \snakecase{stern_layer_thickness}.

Resolution with this mesh type is controlled through \snakecase{scale}. Its value is equal to the inverse resolution in Ångström, meaning that a value of 2 corresponds to a resolution of 0.5 \,\AA{}. This resolution only applies to the fine region.

Lastly, there was also the undocumented parameter \snakecase{loc_refinement}, which could basically force the mesh to be refined regardless of \snakecase{mesh_shape}, and thus route the energy calculation through the \snakecase{energy} function instead of \snakecase{energy_fast}.

\subsubsection{Simulation Parameters}

In this section, users can set the physical properties of the system. 
First and foremost are the dielectric constants of the molecule and the solvent relative to vacuum permittivity $\varepsilon_0$.
Then come the solvent properties like ionic strength and temperature.
It also offers different boundary conditions, which tell the system how the electrostatic potential has to behave at the edge of the system.
Lastly, it controls which energies get computed. For my purposes I enabled \snakecase{calc_coulombic} and set \snakecase{calc_energy} to 2, meaning it computes all three energies.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Parameters}

Here the user may choose between LIS and MUMPS, and choosing the former also allows for more detailed options.
By default it selects LIS with the following settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item linear solver: CGS
    \item preconditioner: SSOR (Symmetric successive over-relaxation)
    \item accuracy: 1.e-6
\end{itemize}
Oddly enough, MUMPS is only mentioned in the references of the NGPB paper, while LIS is mentioned at least once.

\subsubsection{PQR files}

PQR is the standard file format used by NGPB and also APBS for describing biomolecular structures.
They contain the position of the atoms (p), partial charges (q) and atomic radii (r), hence the name.
This is all the necessary information required for the computation.

\subsubsection{PDB and mmCIF files}

PDB is a legacy file format used by the protein data bank, a data base that catalogues proteins, which gives them their characteristic ID.
They only contain the atomic coordinates, as well as residue and chain information.
They're missing the partial charges and atomic radii, and require additional SIZ and CRG files for radii and charges to work inside NGPB.
Alternatively, they can be converted to PQR using \snakecase{pdb2pqr} from the APBS toolset.

PDB have a 5 digit limit for the number of atoms, meaning the largest model it could represent would be 99 999 atoms big.
To solve this limitation, a new file mmCIF or PDBx type was introduced.
As of now, \snakecase{pdb2pqr} is not capable of converting this new file type correctly. 
Researchers work around this by converting to mmCIF to PDB first, which means that for structures larger than 99 999 atoms like 1vsz all indices after that lose their last digit.
Fortunately NGPB does not care about these indices and simply gives them their own as it goes through the file line by line.

\subsection{Grid \& Surface Construction}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-grid-surface-construction}

As mentioned in \ref{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-input}, I will focus solely on the de-refined mesh (\snakecase{mesh_shape = 0}).
NGPB uses p8est to construct the octree, where the fine region is nested inside the corse region.
It places the molecule inside the fine region so that \snakecase{perfil1}
is satisfied for every dimension.
In a sense this fine region retains the aspect ratio of the molecule.
The coarse region on the other hand cannot always satisfy \snakecase{perfil2} exactly, because the octree can only bisect.
The finest unrefined cell $size$ has to be $1/scale$.
An octree can only bisect, so at level-L the size is equal to $1/2^L$.
This is why $size$ gets doubled until $l_{max}/size < perfil2$ is satisfied, where $l_{max}$ is the longest side of the molecule.
This also means that the coarse region is always cubic.
P4est maintains a 2:1 level ratio between the fine region and the coarse region, resulting in a transition region, which is always situated outside the fine region.
This is standard practice with octrees and avoids quadrants that share no vertices with their immediate neighbors.

After the grid construction, NGPB computes the surface of the molecule \snakecase{init_analytical_surf_ns}.
It does this with NanoShaper, which casts rays in the fine region using the same $1/scale$ grid.
This also initializes the ray cache.
Next it constructs the ``epsilon map'' $\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:dielectric-step-function} for the entire space inside \snakecase{create_markers}.

\subsubsection{Ray Cache}

This is a central component that gets used throughout multiple stages.
As its name suggests it holds the information about the rays cast by NanoShaper.
It stores a list of intersections and normals as an object \snakecase{crossing_t} under coordinates on an axis plane. 
The first index for the outermost array is the axis, so in a 3D environment those are the axes x, y, and z. 
The next two for the binary tree map are the coordinates on the perpendicular plane along said axis.
The intersections are then the coordinates along the axis, which together with the coordinates on the plane gives you the point of each intersection.
Normals are triplets ordered by the intersection.
Lastly, \snakecase{crossing_t} also has an \snakecase{init} flag, symbolizing whether the ray has to be recast.
This is a very efficient representation that is unfortunately not portable to the GPU, due to the binary tree map, whose values are complex structs, not simple types.

It is initialized in the method \snakecase{init_analytical_surf_ns} from the rays cast by NanoShaper during the surface construction. 
If NGPB is run on multiple MPI ranks, the ray cache is only initialized on rank 0's, leaving it empty on the other ranks.
Those are later filled in \snakecase{create_markers} through method \snakecase{fill_cache}, which scatters rays from the rank 0 cache to the others. 

Additional rays can be recast individually using \snakecase{compute_ns_inters} or in a batch using \snakecase{compute_pending_rays}.
The latter goes through the cached rays and uses the \snakecase{init} flag of \snakecase{crossing_t} to determine whether they should be recomputed.
\snakecase{create_markers} can also call it implicitly if requested rays are missing from the cache or are uninitialized.

\subsubsection{create\_markers}

This function classifies quadrants as either being inside, outside or on the border of the molecule, and also sets their respective permittivity in the map.
Its also redistributes of ray information to all ranks, and casts additional rays if needed.
For rank 0, it checks whether rays are present in the ray cache using \snakecase{is_in_ns_surf}, which casts a new ray on a miss.
Additional rays may also be cast for refined meshes, which have quadrants that are smaller than $1/scale$.
For the remaining ranks, it collects a list of rays that it needs.
Rank 0 then distributes those rays to the corresponding ranks using \snakecase{fill_cache}.

\subsection{Matrix Assembly}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-matrix-assembly}

In this step NGPB constructs the matrix operator and right-hand-side (RHS) of the LPBE. 
This is done using \subsubsection{assemble\_system\_matrix} and \snakecase{create\_density\_map} respectively.

\subsubsection{create\_density\_map}

This method computes the RHS of the LPBE from the fixed charge densities.
It calls \snakecase{bim3a_solution_with_ghosts} twice.

\subsubsection{assemble\_system\_matrix}

This method computes the differential operator of the LPBE.
It allocates the CSR matrix and computes the operator using \snakecase{bim3a_laplacian_frac} in combination with \snakecase{cube_fraction_intersection}.
It is by far the most memory-intensive step, due to BIMPP's architectural choices explained in \ref{sec:nextgenpb-libraries-bimpp}.
This step also applies the boundary condition.

\subsubsection{cube\_fraction\_intersection}

This method computes the fraction of each edge that is inside the molecule by looking up the intersections of edge-aligned rays from the ray cache.
It then takes the last intersection point it finds and computes its length relative to the starting edge vertex, which it then divides by the total edge length.

\subsection{Solving the Linear Equation}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-solve-linear-equation}

In this step the linear PBE is solved for the electrostatic potential.
Depending on which solver is selected in the PRM file, this is either done with \snakecase{lis_compute_electric_potential} or \snakecase{mumps_compute_electric_potential}.
Relying heavily on matrix operations, this step is a great candidate for GPU acceleration.
Other solver libraries can be easily inserted here.

\subsection{Energy Calculation}
\label{sec:nextgenpb-submodules-energy-calculation}

This step finally computes the three energies, as well as the total energy of the system.
Depending on whether the mesh has been refined, NGPB chooses between two sets of methods.
Methods suffixed by ``fast'' assume that the mesh is unrefined, and as the name implies, skips certain steps that would be needed if it were.
The main two methods here are \snakecase{energy} and \snakecase{energy_fast}, which compute the energies according to the following formulas:

\subsubsection{Coulomb Energy}

The Coulomb energy is the typical interaction between two charged particles $q_i$ and $q_j$ without self-interaction, so $\lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j \rVert$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Coulomb-energy} never equals 0. 
\begin{equation}
    E_{coul} = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{atoms}} \sum_{j<i} \frac{q_i q_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_m \lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j \rVert}
    \label{eq:Coulomb-energy}
\end{equation}
This formulation already avoids unnecessary interactions, by essentially evaluating only the triangle of the matrix spanned by the $N \times N$ interaction.

\subsubsection{Polarization Energy}

The polarization energy arises from polarization charges at the surface where the dielectric constant jumps. It is always evaluated on the intersection point $\mathbf{r}_p$ between the surface and the grid.
The authors approximate the electric flux along a surface patch with:
\begin{equation}
    D_v^{eff} = - \varepsilon^{eff}(\phi_2 - \phi_1)/h_v
    \label{eq:electric-flux}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon^{eff}$ is the WHA according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective-permittivity}.
It uses \snakecase{classifyCube_flux(_fast)} to gather the necessary values per quadrant.
The total polarization charge is computed with:
\begin{equation}
    q_p \approx \varepsilon_0 \left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon_s} - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_m} \right) D_v^{eff} h_v^2
    \label{eq:polarization-charge}
\end{equation}
where $h_v^2$ is the area of the surface patch associated with the intersection point.
Summing the interaction of every such polarization charge with every atomic charge then gives the polarization energy:
\begin{equation}
    E_{pol} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{atoms}} \sum_{p=1}^{N_{Ic}} \frac{q_i q_p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \lVert \mathbf{r}_p - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert}
    \label{eq:polarization-energy}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{classifyCube\_flux(\_fast)}

This method returns the $\varepsilon$ and $\phi$ for each quadrant's node.
Because all fine region quadrants are at $1/scale$ when no refinement is done, the \snakecase{fast} version skips checking for hanging nodes.
Additionally it computes the flux direction along the edges, on which the $\varepsilon$ changes.

\subsubsection{Ionic Energy}

The ionic energy is generated by the interaction between the solvent counterions and the charges of the solvent, and it is very small compared to the polarization and Coulomb energies.
Computing it would require doing an expensive volume integration over the entire solvent volume, which is why it was either avoided, computed implicitly by evaluating the total energy directly, or computed indirectly through multiple runs with different ionic strengths.
However, using Green's identities, the authors of NGPB converted the volume integral into a surface integral over $\Gamma$.
\begin{equation}
    E_{ion} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{atoms}} q_i \left( \int_{\Gamma} \phi(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}) \frac{(\tilde{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{r}_i) \cdot \mathbf{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}})}{4\pi \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert^3} \, dS - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_s} \int_{\Gamma} \frac{\mathbf{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}) \cdot \mathbf{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{r}})}{4\pi \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{r}} - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert} \, dS \right)
    \label{eq:ionic-energy}
\end{equation}
As stated in the NGPB paper, the second integral is the same, and instead of recomputing, the intermediate result from the polarization energy calculation gets reused.
The first integral, denoted $I$, is discretized using triangles instead of surface points.
To get these triangles, NGPB uses \snakecase{classifyCube(_fast)}, which uses marching cubes to locally re-triangulate the surface.
Let $N_t$ be the totality of all triangles.
Then the first integral can be computed discretely as follows:
\begin{equation}
    I \approx \sum_{j=1}^{N_t} \frac{T_j}{3} \sum_{k=A,B,C} \phi(\mathbf{r}_k) \frac{(\mathbf{r}_k - \mathbf{r}_i) \cdot \mathbf{n}(\mathbf{r}_k)}{4\pi \lVert \mathbf{r}_k - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert^3}
    \label{eq:ionic-energy-discrete}
\end{equation}
where $T_j$ is the area of the $j$-th triangle and $A$, $B$ and $C$ are the three corner nodes.
The normals are taken from the ray cache using \snakecase{normal_intersection}, which, it turns out, is not always there.
In very rare cases, NanoShaper and marching cubes disagree on where the surface is exactly.
Marching cubes can only find triangles whose corners always lie in the middle of the quadrant's edges.
In theory, NanoShaper's rays are aligned with those edges and should thus find the intersections along them.
However, if marching cubes finds a triangle where NanoShaper has not found any intersections, it also will not have any normal vector for it.

\subsubsection{classifyCube(\_fast)}

This method computes the triangulation for a cube using the marching cubes algorithm.
The value it returns is an 8-bit marching cube index.
It is exclusively used for the ionic energy calculation.
Just like in \snakecase{classifyCube_flux(_fast)}, the \snakecase{fast} version also ignores hanging nodes.

\subsubsection{normal\_intersection}

This method fetches the intersection point and normal on an edge by looking for a ray in the ray cache which is exactly aligned with it.
It then searches for intersections between the edge start node and the end node.
It is an impure function, writing both its answers to a pointer supplied to it.

\subsubsection{Total Energy}

The total energy is then simply the sum of all contributions:
\begin{equation}
    E_{tot} = E_{coul} + E_{pol} + E_{ion}
    \label{eq:total-energy}
\end{equation}
However, the computation of the Coulomb energy and the ionic energy can be disabled individually through \snakecase{calc_coulombic} and \snakecase{calc_energy}.
In that case the total energy would only be the result of the polarization energy $E_{pol}$, which is the only term that cannot be individually toggled.
There is also the option to not compute the energy at all.
