\section{Poisson Boltzmann Equation}
\label{sec:background-poisson-boltzmann-equation}

The Poisson Boltzmann Equation (PBE) is a non-linear differential equation that solves the mean field electrostatic potential generated by a distribution of fixed charges in a dielectric medium immersed in another one.
It is a crucial instrument for modeling interactions at the molecular level, and is used throughout various scientific fields like biomolecular chemistry, soft matter physics and colloidal science.
In the context of biomolecular chemistry, the fixed point charges represent biochemical molecules such as proteins, while the immersive dielectric medium represents some solvent containing mobile ions.
For instance this could be viral protein submersed in water.

I will start out explaining the PBE same way the authors of NextGenPB \cite{diflorio2025nextgenpb} did, by defining the bounded domain $D$.
It consists of the molecule $\Omega_m$, the solute $\Omega_s$ and the interface or surface between both subdomains $\Gamma$.
The latter is formed by modeling the atoms of the molecule as spheres, which may overlap, as can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:domain-schematic}.

    \centering
    \input{figures/domain_schematic.tex}
    \caption[Mathematical model of a solvated biomolecule]{Mathematical model of a solvated biomolecule: interior (molecule) domain $\Omega_m$, exterior (solvent) domain $\Omega_s$, and the interface $\Gamma$ (solvent excluded surface) separating them. Own illustration.}
    \label{fig:domain-schematic}
\end{figure}

From here the change in dielectric permittivity between the molecule and solvent can be modeled as a step function:
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(\mathbf{r}) =
    \begin{cases}
        \varepsilon_m = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_{r,m} & \text{if } \mathbf{r} \in \Omega_m \\
        \varepsilon_s = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_{r,s} & \text{if } \mathbf{r} \in \Omega_s
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:dielectric-step-function}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity (also called the dielectric constant), and $\varepsilon_m$ and $\varepsilon_s$ are the permittivities of the molecule and solvent.

In its basic form, the PBE essentially says that the divergence of the gradient of the electrostatic potential $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ within the molecule and the solute is equal to their charge densities $\rho_m$ and $\rho_s$:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \cdot \left( \varepsilon(\mathbf{r}) \nabla \phi(\mathbf{r}) \right) = -\rho_m(r) - \rho_s(r) \qquad \mathbf{r} \in D
    \label{eq:pbe-basic}
\end{equation}
The electric potential $\varepsilon(\mathbf{r})$ is a scalar field spanning across the domain $D$. 
Calculating its gradient $\nabla \phi(\mathbf{r})$ results in a vector field describing the directional changes of the potential, which is the electric field.
Multiplied with the permittivity, it becomes the electric flux density, which measures the strength of the electric displacement.
Taking the divergence of it gives yet another scalar field describing the flow.
To summarize: wherever the electric flux density diverges, the charge density is high, and vice versa.

The charge density in the molecule is composed of fixed charges, that are $N$ point charges $q_i$ at position $r_i$:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_m(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N q_i \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_i)
    \label{eq:charge-density-molecule}
\end{equation}

The non-linearity lies within the charge density of the solvent.
It consists of the valence and bulk ions concentrations, $z_j$ and $\rho_j^b$, of $J$ different ionic species multiplied by the Boltzmann weight:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_s(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{j=1}^J e z_j \rho_j^b \exp\left[-\frac{e z_j \phi(\mathbf{r})}{K_b T}\right] \chi_{\Omega_s}(\mathbf{r})
    \label{eq:charge-density-solvent}
\end{equation}
$K_b$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the temperature and $\chi_{\Omega_s}$ is a characteristic function, which equals 1 when $r \in \Omega_s$ and is otherwise 0.
Putting it all together yields the full PBE:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \cdot \left( \varepsilon(\mathbf{r}) \nabla \phi(\mathbf{r}) \right) = - \sum_{i=1}^N q_i \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_i) - \sum_{j=1}^J e z_j \rho_j^b \exp\left[-\frac{e z_j \phi(\mathbf{r})}{K_b T}\right] \chi_{\Omega_s}(\mathbf{r})
    \label{eq:pbe-full}
\end{equation}

The de-facto linearized version of the PBE is the Debye-Hückel theorem, which assumes that $K_b T \gg e z_j \phi(\mathbf{r})$, removing the exponential term from Eq.~\ref{eq:charge-density-solvent} and replacing it with the Debye factor:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_D^2 = \frac{e^2}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_{r,s} K_b T} \sum_{j=1}^J \rho_j^b z_j^2
    \label{eq:debye-factor}
\end{equation}
such that the charge density is now approximated by:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_s(\mathbf{r}) \approx \kappa_D^2 \chi_{\Omega_s}(\mathbf{r}) \phi(\mathbf{r})
    \label{eq:approx-charge-density-solvent}
\end{equation}
This leads to the linear, elliptic partial differential equation:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla \cdot \left( \varepsilon(\mathbf{r}) \nabla \phi(\mathbf{r}) \right) = - \sum_{i=1}^N q_i \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_i) - \kappa_D^2 \chi_{\Omega_s}(\mathbf{r}) \phi(\mathbf{r})
    \label{eq:lpbe}
\end{equation}

\subsection{PBE solvers}
\label{sec:background-poisson-boltzmann-equation-pbe-solvers}

As it is a very important scientific instrument, many numerical solvers have been developed for both the LPBE and the regular PBE.
There are two main types of solvers: the grid-based or finite element methods (FEM) and the boundary integral or boundary element methods (BEM) methods \cite{geng2013tabipb}.

FEM discretize the entire domain and thus do not need to reformulate the PBE, nor do they restricted to the LPBE.
Naturally their ability to capture the surface depends on the resolution of the grid, measured in Ångström.
Increase in accuracy is also non-monotonic due to grid-position artifacts.
Using a uniform grid is often wasteful as high resolution is not needed in the solvent domain $\Omega_s$.
For this reason, these solvers employ de-refinement of the grid to capture the surface $\Gamma$ and the molecule domain $\Omega_m$, while keeping resource utilization as low as possible.
They necessarily compute the electrostatic potential for the entire domain $D$, and not just on the surface.
Their linear equation contains a sparse matrix, which is a differential operator
The far-field boundary condition is often satisfied approximately.

BEM reformulate the LPBE as boundary integral at the surface $\Gamma$ by applying Green's identities, and discretize over that.
Thus they can only compute the electrostatic potential at the surface $\Gamma$, but it also reduces the memory utilization when compared to FEM.
They are also forced to use the LPBE, since the reformulation depends on it.
Their accuracy depends on the number of triangles used to discretize the surface, making its increase monotonic.
They solve a linear system with a dense matrix, that is integral operator.
The far-field boundary condition is exactly satisfied at spatial infinity.

Solvers need to avoid the so called self-energy singularity, which is an artifact of the point charge model, where the electrostatic potential explodes when it is evaluated at the position of the charge.
Looking at Eq~\ref{eq:coulomb-potential} for the basic Coulomb potential, one can identify the problem, that if $\mathbf{r_i}$ in the $\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_i$ term equals $\mathbf{r}$, the divisor drops to 0 and the potential diverges to infinity.
\begin{equation}
    \varphi(\mathbf{r}_i) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon \lVert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_i \rVert}
    \label{eq:coulomb-potential}
\end{equation}

BEM avoid it intrinsically, since they only evaluate the potential at the surface.
FEM need to come up with different ways of getting around it.
Many simply run the simulation twice with different parameters to compute the ``grid energies'' and use it to cancel out the self-energies.
Another way is to replace the point charge model with finite-width Gaussians.
NextGenPB employs a new strategy, that computes each electrostatic contribution individually, each avoiding the self-energy singularity.

\section{Message Passing Interface}
\label{sec:background-mpi}

The message piping interface (MPI) is an API standard for data communication between processes, available for multiple different programming languages \cite{mpi_forum_standard}.
It is not a single library.
Rather it has many different versions and implementations of standard specifications set by the MPI Forum, which is an open consortium of vendors researcher and national labs.
It is conceptualized for clusters with multiple compute nodes, and distributed memory architectures, but it is very flexible and works with all kinds of different architectures, even heterogenous ones.
On single machine, it can still provide quasi-multicore parallelization, though without shared memory.
For this use case another API called OpenMP is the better choice \cite{openmp_spec}.

Programs utilizing MPI need to be compiled with the language-appropriate compiler.
In the case of C++ this is either \texttt{mpiCC} or \texttt{mpicxx}.
Running these programs also requires the use of the \texttt{mpirun} or \texttt{mpiexec} command.
Each process is called a rank, and their number can be specified by passing the \texttt{-n} when launching a program.
Communication between then is done through specialized functions and types provided by an MPI library.

\section{CUDA}
\label{sec:background-cuda}

At this point it is common knowledge that GPUs offer many more, albeit less capable compute units than CPUs.
Intended for rendering 3D graphics, GPUs specialized on handling many vector and matrix operations in parallel.
Nvidia's CUDA API was born out the desire to leverage the highly parallel architecture for applications outside of graphics rendering.
Stanford researcher Ian Buck exploited graphics APIs to do scientific math calculations, before being picked up by Nvidia in 2004 to work on what would become CUDA \cite{nemire2015gpuevolution}.
However, it was not just the software that changed.
GPU architectures themselves changed as well to handle general-purpose compute rather than focusing solely on graphics.
After its introduction in 2007, CUDA had quickly become an important part of Nvidia's business strategy, as it became dominant in the space of massively-parallel computing.

It is important to point out that CUDA is a proprietary API, which only works with Nvidia's GPUs.
Luckily this applies to both gaming or consumer grade, and workstation GPUs, although there are slight differences.
General-purpose GPU computing is not a silver bullet.
A problem needs to be parallelizable to really take advantage of the architecture.
Strictly sequential problems, and those that are very limited in how much they can be parallelized, are still better served on the CPU. 

\subsection{GPU Architecture}
\label{sec:background-cuda-gpu-architecture}
I will use Nvidia's Ampere architecture to give a basic overview, as it is the main architecture used for the evaluation of this thesis.
Other generations might differ in certain areas, especially in the specific number of units, so I will omit them from my explanation.

The Ampere GPU architecture \cite{nvidia_ampere_ga102_whitepaper} is organized in groups of Streaming Multiprocessors (SM) called Graphics Processing Clusters (GPC), connected through the L2-Cache (Figure~\ref{fig:ga102-full-gpu}).
SMs (Figure~\ref{fig:ga10x-sm}) are then partitioned into processing blocks, each containing multiple CUDA cores, and a register file.
Each SM also had one L1-Cache and FP64 units, which are absent from diagram.

Even among the same architecture generation the number of FP64 units varies drastically between chips intended for workstations and those intended for gaming.
While the GA102 chip intended for gaming only has two FP64 units per SM, the ``full scale'' GA100 chip, from which the GA102 is derived, features 32.
This results in a FP32 to FP64 performance ratio of 2:1 on GA100.
The GA102 features double the amount of FP32 cores, which does mean its FP32 performance is higher than GA100, but also exacerbates the performance ration even further to 64:1.
This is a cost saving measure, as video games, unlike scientific applications, rarely need FP64 operations.
However, Nvidia also markets GPUs like the RTX Pro series as workstation GPUs, even though they are based on the gaming chip, and thus have the same cut-down FP64 performance.

Video memory, or VRAM is absent from the GPU chip, instead being placed around it on the PCB and connected through the memory controllers.
Ordered by descending transfer speed, the hierarchy of the memory is as follows: First comes the register file of the processing blocks, then the L1-Cache of the SM, then the L2-Cache and finally the VRAM.

From the standpoint of the CUDA API, the architecture appears slightly differently.
The L2-Cache and VRAM become opaque, and are treated as one ``global memory''.
The SM is abstracted as a ``block'' and its L1-Cache as the ``shared memory'.

\subsection{Basic Overview}
\label{sec:background-cuda-basic-overview}

In the context of the API the CPU and GPU are referred to as the host and the device.
The API allows users to program custom programs called kernels, which are launched on one or more devices.
Device memory is separate from the host memory.
Due to this, host data cannot be directly passed to the kernels, and kernels cannot return values directly, and thus needs to be copied back and forth between those.
The basic flow of a CUDA program is typically structured like this \cite{nvidia_cuda_programming_guide}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Allocation of device memory, both for input and output
    \item Copying of host memory into the allocated device input memory
    \item Launching of the kernel
    \item Copying of the device output memory
\end{itemize}
Data in the allocated device memory lives primarily in the global memory.
Kernels are launched as a number of threads per block.

\subsection{Compiling}
\label{sec:background-cuda-compiling}

CUDA source files, signified by \texttt{*.cu}, are compiled using the \texttt{nvcc} compiler, which wraps around an existing c++ compiler \cite{nvidia_cuda_programming_guide}.
It automatically differentiates between host and device code, so both code types can live in the same file.
The host code is compiled using the c++ compiler, while the device code is compiled with \texttt{nvcc}.

Though CUDA and MPI each use their own compilers, combining CUDA with MPI is possible with a couple of extra steps.
A guide on the RWTH web page \cite{rwth_cuda} details, that one needs to compile the CUDA source files to object files using \texttt{nvcc} first, before linking them during the compilation of the remaining code with \texttt{mpicxx}.
The necessary CUDA libraries need to be linked as well.
This not only enables parallelism on the CPU side, but also scaling across multiple compute nodes, unlocking the power of GPU enabled computer clusters.

\subsection{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:background-cuda-performance-evaluation}

GPUs have essentially two major performance limits, those being the compute and memory throughput.
People often think of theoretical peak performance as just the compute throughput in the measure of floating point operations per second, or $FLOP/s$, but performance can only be realized if the data can be supplied fast enough to saturate it.
This is the memory throughput and it is measured in $Bytes/s$.
When plotting both metrics against each other, we get was is called a ``roofline model'', which symbolizes the limit on the theoretical performance.
The exactly values of the diagram vary with the capability of the GPU, but the shape always stays the same.
Kernels always lie underneath the roofline and its position gives us an indication of how well optimized it is, and what its main limit is.
Kernels to the left of the ridge are bound by memory-throughput, while kernels to the right are bound by the compute-throughput.
The close a kernel is to the roofline, the more optimized it is.

The roofline, as well as, many more performance metrics can be easily evaluated thanks to Nsight Compute software (NCU), Nvidia's own profiler.
To record a profile, one must simply run the application through the \texttt{ncu} command line tool.
It automatically recognizes the CUDA kernels, but there also options to flag which kernels should be profiled inside the code.
The profiles can then be inspected in the GUI application.

\section{Sparse Matrices}
\label{sec:background-sparse-matrices}

Often times matrices describing 2D or 3D systems have many zero-entires in them.
In a dense matrix all of the values are stored, meaning those zeroes contribute to the memory footprint.
This severely limits the matrix size that could realistically computed.
Sparse matrices mitigate this problem by storing only non-zero values.
There are several techniques to store them.

One easily modifiable type is die dictionary of keys (DOK), which stores the non-zero values, keyed by the two coordinates of their position within the dense matrix.
Entries missing from the dictionary are implicitly considered 0.
Since they are implemented using hash tables or maps, they have some amount of memory overhead, and the way they store their values is inefficient for matrix multiplications.

Conversely, Compressed Sparse Row format utilize 3 arrays: column indices and values with the length of the non-zero-values (NNZ), and row offsets with the length equal to the number of rows plus one.
Figure~\ref{fig:csr-format} shows an example of a small $4\times4$ sparse matrix and its corresponding CSR representation.
The \texttt{values} array stores the non-zero entries in row-major order, and \texttt{col\_idx} stores the column each of those entries belongs to.
\texttt{row\_offsets} then stores, for each row, the index into \texttt{values}/\texttt{col\_idx} at which that row's entries begin, with a final trailing entry equal to NNZ so that the length of any row can be computed as \texttt{row\_offsets[i+1] - row\_offsets[i]}.
On the surface this might look more complicated than a DOK but the CSR wins when accessing all row values of a matrix, which is conducive to matrix multiplication.
Since \texttt{row\_offsets} depends on the number of values per row, it needs to be recomputed every time values are added or removed.

\section{Linear Equation Solvers}
\label{sec:background-linear-eq-solvers}

In this section I want to go over the different types of methods for solving linear equations in the form of $Ax = b$, where $A$ is a square matrix, and $x$ and $b$ are vectors. 
$b$ is commonly referred to as the right-hand-side (RHS) which is known, as it is the vector $x$ that these solvers are computing.

\subsection{Direct Solvers}
\label{sec:background-linear-eq-solvers-direct-solvers}

One main type of solver is the direct solver.
They compute the exact solution in a predictable sequence of steps.
They utilize methods like LU- or Cholesky decomposition to break the matrix $A$ down into triangular matrix components in order to simplify the computation.

The have one very big downside though: high memory usage.
They need to store the matrices resulting from the factorization.
Even using sparse matrices this increases the storage complexity from \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(n^{4/3})\) where $n$ is the number of unknowns in a 3D mesh.
On the other hand they scale very well on highly parallel architectures.

\subsection{Iterative Solvers}
\label{sec:background-linear-eq-solvers-iterative-solvers}

The other main type of solver is the iterative solver.
Their basic idea is to start with an approximation and converges towards the solution with each iteration, until the desired accuracy or residual error is reached.

The largest family of iterative solver methods are the so called Krylov methods, which use the name-giving Krylov subspace to appropriate the solution \cite{anzt2015gpukrylov}.
With a starting vector $x_0$ they compute the initial residual $r_0$ with:

\begin{equation}
    r_0 = b - A x_0
    \label{eq:initial-residual}
\end{equation}

They then construct the Krylov subspace through projections of a vector $r_0$ through increasing powers of the matrix $A$.

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K}_i(A, r_0) = \mathrm{span}\{r_0, A r_0, A^2 r_0, \dots, A^{i-1} r_0\}
    \label{eq:krylov-subspace}
\end{equation}

In which the look for a vector $z_i$, which is added to $x_0$, which is then used to compute the next residual.
It is proven that outside of rounding errors, this method terminates at a finite number of steps \cite{saad2003iterative}.

Accuracy does not depend on the choice of iterative solver.
This only influences the amount of iterations.
Naturally their performance is governed by the number of iterations needed multiplied by the time each iteration takes.
Although they do not provide an exact solution like the direct solvers, they utilizes a lot less memory, needing little more or with some methods, no extra memory other than the original matrix itself.

Traditional Krylov methods suffer from a strong-scaling wall, due to a global reduction step present at either the construction of the Krylov space, or computation of $z_i$.
For this purpose there exist so called ``communication-avoidant'' Krylov methods.
